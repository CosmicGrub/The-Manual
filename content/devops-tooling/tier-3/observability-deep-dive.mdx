## Where Tier 2 left off

In Tier 2's basic observability module you got a service emitting metrics, a Prometheus server scraping them, and probably a log aggregator tailing stdout into something searchable. That's the minimum viable setup — it tells you a service is running and lets you grep for errors after the fact. This module is about what you do once "it's running" isn't a good enough answer anymore: when you have five services calling each other, a latency spike that only shows up under load, and an on-call rotation that's three alerts away from muting your pager entirely.

Everything here builds directly on that Tier 2 foundation. You're not replacing Prometheus — you're writing PromQL that actually answers questions. You're not replacing your logs — you're adding the piece that connects a log line in service A to a log line in service B. And you're not adding more alerts — you're fixing the ones that are currently training your team to ignore Slack notifications.

## The three pillars: what each one answers that the others can't

"Metrics, logs, and traces" gets repeated so often it turns into a slogan nobody unpacks. Here's the unpacked version, with a single incident walking through all three.

**Metrics** answer: *what is the aggregate behavior of the system right now, and is it different from normal?* A metric is a number with labels, sampled over time — request count, error count, CPU percent, queue depth. Metrics are cheap to store and fast to query precisely because they throw away the details of any individual event. `http_requests_total{status="500"}` doesn't know which user hit the error or what their cart contained — it just knows a 500 happened, and it can tell you instantly whether that's happening 1 time a day or 1 time a second. This is why metrics are what you alert on: they're the only pillar cheap enough to evaluate every 15 seconds, forever, across your whole fleet.

**Logs** answer: *what exactly happened, in this one case?* A log line is a structured (hopefully) record of a specific event, with whatever detail the developer decided to include — the stack trace, the user ID, the exact SQL that failed, the request body. Logs are where you go once a metric tells you something is wrong, to find out *which* thing and *why*. They don't scale to "query across my whole system in real time" the way metrics do — searching terabytes of logs is slow and expensive — and a log line from the checkout service has no inherent connection to a log line from the inventory service, even if they were both caused by the same user request.

**Traces** answer: *where did the time go, and which hop in a multi-service call caused it?* A trace is the thing that stitches together the checkout service's log line and the inventory service's log line, by tagging every piece of work caused by one incoming request with a shared trace ID. No amount of metrics or logs, read separately, tells you that 3.8 of the 4 seconds your `/checkout` endpoint took were spent waiting on a downstream gRPC call — you need something that understands causal, cross-service structure.

Walk it through: a user reports checkout is slow. Metrics show it immediately — p99 latency on `/checkout` jumped from 300ms to 4.1s at 14:32, and it's *only* p99, so it's not everyone, just the tail. Logs from the checkout service show nothing — no errors, no exceptions, just normal entries. If you stopped at logs and metrics, you'd be stuck. The trace for a slow request shows a waterfall: `POST /checkout` (4.1s total) → `validate_cart` (40ms) → `charge_card` (120ms) → `reserve_inventory` (3.8s) → `send_confirmation` (60ms). Now you know exactly where to look: the inventory service, during that window. Its logs (now that you know when and where to look) show a query doing a full table scan because an index got dropped in a migration. Three pillars, three different questions, one incident.

## Prometheus + Grafana: queries that answer something

Tier 2 had you scraping a `/metrics` endpoint. Assume your checkout service now exposes the two metrics you'll actually use for everything that follows:

```
# A counter, labeled by method/path/status
http_requests_total{job="checkout-service", method="POST", path="/checkout", status="200"} 48213

# A histogram, which Prometheus turns into _bucket, _sum, and _count series
http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="checkout-service", path="/checkout", le="0.1"} 41022
http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="checkout-service", path="/checkout", le="0.5"} 47890
http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="checkout-service", path="/checkout", le="1.0"} 48110
http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="checkout-service", path="/checkout", le="+Inf"} 48213
```

A raw counter value is useless on its own — "48213" tells you nothing. The query that matters is the *rate of change*:

```promql
sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-service"}[5m])) by (path)
```

`rate()` looks at the increase in the counter over the trailing 5-minute window and divides by time, giving you requests-per-second, correctly handling counter resets (a deploy restarts the process, the counter goes back to 0, `rate()` doesn't report a nonsensical negative spike). `sum(...) by (path)` collapses the per-instance series into one line per endpoint, so a 10-pod deployment gives you one graph, not ten.

The query that actually tells you something is broken is error *ratio*, not error *count*:

```promql
sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-service", status=~"5.."}[5m]))
/
sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-service"}[5m]))
```

5 errors a minute is a crisis at 10 requests/minute (50% failure) and a rounding error at 50,000 requests/minute. Dividing by total request rate is what makes the number meaningful regardless of traffic volume, and it's the query you'll reuse for the alert later in this module.

For latency, use the histogram with `histogram_quantile`:

```promql
histogram_quantile(0.95,
  sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="checkout-service", path="/checkout"}[5m])) by (le)
)
```

This reads as: "of all checkout requests in the last 5 minutes, 95% completed faster than this value." Grouping `by (le)` (the bucket upper bound label) is required — without it, `histogram_quantile` can't reconstruct the distribution.

**Building the panel in Grafana:** create a new panel, paste the error-ratio query as query A, set the panel's unit to **Percent (0.0-1.0)** (Grafana then renders `0.05` as `5%` instead of asking you to multiply by 100 in PromQL), and set visualization to Time series. Add two threshold lines under the panel's Thresholds tab: yellow at `0.01` (1%), red at `0.05` (5%). Add a second query B for the p95 latency expression on the same panel's second axis, or — better — a separate panel next to it, since error rate and latency are different units and conflating them on one axis hides both. That's the dashboard: one row showing request rate, error ratio, and p95 latency, side by side, all driven by the two metrics you were already scraping.

## OpenTelemetry: instrumenting one function, seeing one trace

Metrics and logs you likely already have from Tier 2. Traces require actually instrumenting code — wrapping units of work in **spans** that carry a trace ID, so a tracing backend (Jaeger, Tempo, or even the console for learning) can reconstruct the waterfall from the earlier example.

Take the `charge_card` function from the checkout flow and give it a span:

```python
from opentelemetry import trace
from opentelemetry.sdk.trace import TracerProvider
from opentelemetry.sdk.trace.export import BatchSpanProcessor, ConsoleSpanExporter

trace.set_tracer_provider(TracerProvider())
trace.get_tracer_provider().add_span_processor(
    BatchSpanProcessor(ConsoleSpanExporter())
)
tracer = trace.get_tracer("checkout-service")

def charge_card(order_id, amount_usd):
    with tracer.start_as_current_span("charge_card") as span:
        span.set_attribute("order.id", order_id)
        span.set_attribute("charge.amount_usd", amount_usd)
        try:
            result = payment_gateway.charge(order_id, amount_usd)
            span.set_attribute("charge.status", "success")
            return result
        except PaymentError as e:
            span.record_exception(e)
            span.set_status(trace.Status(trace.StatusCode.ERROR, str(e)))
            raise
```

Four things happened here that turn a plain function call into observable work: `start_as_current_span` opens a span that records its own start and end time automatically; `set_attribute` attaches structured context (the order ID, the amount) the way you'd want in a log line, except now it's attached to a specific timed operation instead of floating free; and if the function is itself called from inside another span (say, a parent span around the whole `/checkout` handler), OpenTelemetry automatically makes `charge_card` a *child* of that parent — that parent/child relationship is what produces a waterfall instead of a flat list of unrelated timings.

Running this inside a request that also wraps `validate_cart`, `reserve_inventory`, and `send_confirmation` in their own spans produces, in a trace viewer, exactly the waterfall described earlier:

```
POST /checkout                      [======================] 4.10s
  validate_cart                     [=]                       0.04s
  charge_card                       [==]                       0.12s
  reserve_inventory                 [====================]    3.80s
  send_confirmation                 [=]                        0.06s
```

That picture — which span is the parent, which are siblings, and which one ate the time — is the entire value proposition of tracing, and it's not something a dashboard full of per-service metrics panels can reconstruct, because each service only knows about its own numbers, not the causal chain between them.

## Alerts: the difference between a signal and noise

This is the part of observability most teams get wrong first, and the damage compounds: a bad alert doesn't just waste one person's time, it teaches everyone that alerts are noise, so the one time it's real, it gets acknowledged and ignored along with the rest.

Here's the alert almost everyone writes first:

```yaml
groups:
- name: naive-alerts
  rules:
  - alert: HighErrorRate
    expr: http_requests_total{status="500"} > 0
    for: 0m
    labels:
      severity: critical
    annotations:
      summary: "A 500 error occurred"
```

This looks reasonable and is badly broken, for a reason specific to how Prometheus counters work: `http_requests_total` only ever goes up (it resets to 0 only when the process restarts). The instant your service logs its *first* 500 error — ever, including during initial testing — this expression becomes true, and it stays true for the rest of that process's life, because the counter never goes back down. The alert doesn't fire once; it fires on every evaluation cycle from that point forward, usually every 30-60 seconds, indefinitely. Within a day, on-call has gotten dozens of pages for an error that happened once, hours ago, and is long resolved. The rational response to that is to mute the alert — which is exactly the failure mode: the alert has now trained the team to ignore it, so the *next* time it fires for a genuinely ongoing problem, nobody looks.

Here's the fix, using the same error-ratio query from the Grafana section:

```yaml
groups:
- name: useful-alerts
  rules:
  - alert: CheckoutHighErrorRate
    expr: |
      (
        sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-service", status=~"5.."}[5m]))
        /
        sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="checkout-service"}[5m]))
      ) > 0.05
    for: 10m
    labels:
      severity: critical
    annotations:
      summary: "Checkout error rate above 5% for 10+ minutes"
      description: "Current error rate: {{ $value | humanizePercentage }}. Check recent deploys to checkout-service and the status of its downstream dependencies."
```

Three specific changes, each closing a specific hole:

1. **`rate(...)[5m]` instead of the raw counter.** This measures *recent* error velocity, not lifetime total — a 500 from three days ago has aged out of the window and stopped contributing, so the alert reflects current state, not historical trivia.
2. **A ratio, not a count.** `> 0.05` means "more than 5% of requests are failing," which scales correctly whether you're serving 10 requests/minute or 10,000 — a fixed count threshold (`> 5 errors`) would either never fire at low traffic or fire constantly at high traffic.
3. **`for: 10m` instead of `for: 0m`.** The condition has to stay true continuously for ten minutes before Prometheus fires the alert. A single bad deploy that self-heals in 90 seconds, or one noisy minute during a traffic spike, never reaches the page. Only a *sustained* problem does.

The result is an alert that fires rarely and means something every time it does — which is the entire design goal. Teams that want to go further from here look at multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting (pairing a fast, sensitive window with a slow, high-confidence one, as described in Google's SRE workbook) to catch both fast-burning outages and slow-burning degradations without adding more noisy single-threshold rules — worth knowing exists, not something to hand-build from scratch in this module.

## Exercise

Your `order-processing` service publishes to a queue, and consumers pull from it. You have this metric from the consumer fleet:

```
queue_messages_pending{queue="order-processing"} 142
```

A teammate wrote this alert and it's paging on-call 15 times a day:

```yaml
- alert: QueueBacklog
  expr: queue_messages_pending{queue="order-processing"} > 100
  for: 0m
```

Rewrite it so it only fires on a genuine, sustained backlog — not a normal traffic burst that drains in two minutes. You'll need to think about what "backlog" should actually mean here (a point-in-time depth, or a rate — is the queue draining slower than it's filling?), and pick a `for:` duration that reflects how long a backlog has to persist before it's actually an operational problem rather than normal queueing.

*Hint: consider using `deriv()` or two snapshots of `queue_messages_pending` over a window to check whether the queue is growing, not just whether it's currently above some fixed depth — a queue sitting at 150 but shrinking every minute is healthy; one sitting at 110 and climbing is not.*
