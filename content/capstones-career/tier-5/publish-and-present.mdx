PortWatch-ML exists now. You trained a classifier that scores scan sessions as benign, reconnaissance, or malicious using features pulled straight out of the portwatch.py telemetry you've been collecting since Tier 2 — ports touched, inter-probe timing, SYN/ACK ratios, service-fingerprint entropy. You broke it with a problem-space evasion attack: a timing-and-ordering search that fools the classifier using only moves a real `nmap` invocation can actually make. You hardened it with adversarial training and wired a robustness gate into the Tier 4 CI pipeline that already blocks PRs on SBOM and CVE-severity regressions. That's the work. Nobody knows it exists yet.

This module is about the three things that change that: a writeup, a talk, and an honest accounting of who else has done this. None of them are optional add-ons to "real" work. A capstone nobody can find, verify, or build on didn't close the loop — it just stopped.

## The three outputs, one body of work

The blog post, the talk, and the related-work search aren't three separate projects bolted onto the end of the capstone. They're three views of the same evidence, built for three different failure modes: the blog post fails if it's not scannable by a developer with four minutes; the talk fails if you can't survive a question you didn't rehearse; the related-work section fails if you either claim to have invented adversarial ML or imply your three-week capstone is beneath citing against decade-deep prior work. Get the underlying numbers right once — attack success rate, robust accuracy, perturbation budget, seed count — and all three outputs just reformat the same table for a different reader.

## 1. Writing it up: the blog post version

Dev-audience posts die from buried leads. Here is the structure that survives, with real section content for PortWatch-ML:

**Title:** "I Taught a Classifier to Spot Port Scans, Then Taught an Attacker to Fool It"

**Opening paragraph — the result, not the setup:**

> My port-scan classifier hit 97.8% F1 on held-out traffic from my home-lab sensors. Then I spent two weeks trying to break it. A change to how `nmap` paces its probes — no new packets, no new ports, just slower and reordered — cut recall to 41% without tripping a single existing alert. This post covers building the detector, breaking it on purpose, and the CI gate I added so that doesn't happen silently in production.

That's four sentences and it already contains the whole story. Everything after it is detail, not reveal.

**"Where this started"** — two sentences, not a history lesson: portwatch.py began as a Tier 2 port scanner, grew a CVE-matching layer, then an SBOM/CI-gated scan pipeline in Tier 4. Readers who followed the series get the thread; readers who didn't get just enough to not be lost.

**"The classifier"** — features and baseline, in a table, because developers skim tables and skip prose:

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| `unique_dst_ports` | distinct ports touched in the session window |
| `syn_ack_ratio` | SYN packets sent vs SYN-ACK received |
| `inter_probe_mean_ms`, `inter_probe_var_ms` | timing distribution between probes |
| `port_order_entropy` | how sequential vs randomized the port visitation is |
| `fingerprint_entropy` | diversity of service-banner responses probed |

Model: gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost), chosen over an MLP baseline because the feature set is low-dimensional, tabular, and the team running the CI gate needs to inspect feature importances during incident review, not just trust a black box. Baseline, three-class, held-out test set: 97.8% F1, precision 98.1%, recall 97.5%.

**"Breaking it: two attacks, only one of them honest"**

Run the obvious attack first, as a sanity check, and say so plainly: feature-space PGD directly perturbing the feature vector within an L∞ budget. It works, flips 94% of malicious-labeled sessions to benign at ε=0.3 — and it's a toy result, because nothing guarantees a real scan can produce that exact feature vector. State that limitation in the post itself, immediately, rather than let a reader discover it:

```text
Feature-space PGD, eps=0.3: 94% attack success rate.
Caveat: no check that a perturbed feature vector corresponds
to any packet sequence a real scanner could emit. This number
is a sanity check that the model has exploitable slack, not
a claim about real-world evadability.
```

Then the real attack — problem-space, parameterized over what an attacker actually controls:

```python
import random

def mutate_scan_plan(plan):
    """plan: dict with inter_probe_ms, port_order, decoy_count.
    Returns a neighbor reachable by one realistic nmap change."""
    new_plan = dict(plan)
    move = random.choice(["slow", "shuffle", "decoy"])
    if move == "slow":
        new_plan["inter_probe_ms"] *= random.uniform(1.2, 3.0)
    elif move == "shuffle":
        new_plan["port_order"] = partially_shuffle(plan["port_order"])
    elif move == "decoy":
        new_plan["decoy_count"] = min(plan["decoy_count"] + 1, 8)
    return new_plan

def search_evasion(plan, classifier, simulator, budget=200):
    best = plan
    best_score = classifier.predict_proba(simulator.run(plan))[1]  # P(malicious)
    for _ in range(budget):
        candidate = mutate_scan_plan(best)
        traffic = simulator.run(candidate)        # produces real packet features
        score = classifier.predict_proba(traffic)[1]
        if score < best_score:
            best, best_score = candidate, score
    return best, best_score
```

This is simulated annealing over scan *behavior*, not over the feature vector. The classifier only ever sees features derived from a packet trace the simulator actually produced. That constraint is the whole contribution — more on why in the related-work section.

**Results table, the one that matters:**

| Attack | Budget | Attack success rate | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feature-space PGD | ε=0.3 | 94% | not problem-space realizable |
| Problem-space search | 200 queries, timing+order only | 61% | realizable |
| Problem-space search | 200 queries, + decoys | 78% | realizable |

**"Defending it"** — adversarially trained on problem-space-generated examples mixed into training data, 30% of each batch:

| Model | Clean F1 | Robust F1 (problem-space attack) |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 97.8% | 54.1% |
| Adversarially trained | 93.6% | 81.9% |

State the tradeoff in the same sentence as the win: four points of clean accuracy bought back twenty-eight points of robustness. A reader who's been burned by robustness papers before will trust the post more for showing the cost, not less.

**"Wiring this into CI"** — this is where it connects to Tier 4's existing gate, and the post should say that explicitly rather than re-explain SBOM gating from scratch:

```yaml
# .github/workflows/scan-gate.yml (new step, appended to Tier 4's SBOM gate)
- name: robust-accuracy-gate
  run: |
    python eval_robustness.py --model model.xgb \
      --attack problem_space --budget 200 --min-robust-f1 0.78
```

The PR fails if robust F1 drops below 0.78, same mechanism, same pipeline, as the CVE-severity gate the learner built two tiers ago.

**"What I'd do with more time"** — stated plainly, not buried: only white-box and limited-budget black-box attacks tested, no query-efficient black-box method like HopSkipJump; three random seeds; training and test traffic both drawn from one home-lab/honeypot setup, so generalization to a different network's baseline traffic is untested.

### As an arXiv preprint instead

Same numbers, different contract with the reader. A blog post trusts the reader to skim and trust the author's framing. A preprint reader checks every claim against evidence and against what's already published. That changes the structure, not just the tone:

| Aspect | Blog post | arXiv preprint |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | concrete result, first person, no abstract | 150-250 word Abstract: problem, method, headline result, stated without hedging |
| Structure | narrative: build, break, fix | fixed: Intro, Threat Model, Method, Experimental Setup, Results, Related Work, Limitations, Ethics, Conclusion |
| Threat model | described in a sentence | formalized: attacker knowledge (white/gray/black-box), capability set, perturbation budget defined as a constraint, not prose |
| Related work | linked inline where relevant | dedicated section, every prior paper gets one sentence on how this work differs, not just "see also" |
| Statistics | point estimates (97.8% F1) | multiple seeds, mean ± std or confidence interval, because reviewers will ask |
| Figures | screenshots and inline tables | numbered figures, captions that stand alone without body text, axis units labeled |
| Limitations | one honest closing paragraph | a required section reviewers specifically check for — vague limitations read as a red flag, not humility |
| Reproducibility | a GitHub link | explicit statement: commit hash, hardware, hyperparameter table, seed list, usually in an appendix |
| Ethics | optional | near-mandatory for an attack paper: a dual-use / responsible-disclosure statement, even for a defensive capstone |
| Voice | "I built," "I broke" | "we" even solo, passive constructions tolerated, hedged claims ("suggests" not "proves") |

The single biggest trap going from blog to preprint: carrying over the blog's confident framing into a venue that penalizes it. "Cuts recall to 41%" is a fine blog hook; a preprint abstract needs "reduces recall from 97.5% to 41% under a 200-query problem-space search, averaged over 3 seeds (σ=4.2)" — the same fact, with the uncertainty attached instead of hidden.

## 2. Giving the talk

Timed for a 20-minute slot plus 10 for questions — standard for a capstone defense or local security meetup:

| Time | Content |
|---|---|
| 0:00–1:00 | Live terminal: run the classifier on a real scan, watch it catch it. Then run the evasion attack live, watch it slip through. No slides yet. |
| 1:00–3:30 | One slide: portwatch.py's pipeline, scan → features → CVE match → SBOM/CI gate, with the new ML node added on top. |
| 3:30–8:00 | The classifier: feature table, model choice, baseline numbers. One slide, one table. |
| 8:00–14:00 | The attack: state the threat model out loud (gray-box — attacker knows the feature schema, not the weights), show the problem-space search, show the success-rate-vs-budget plot. |
| 14:00–17:30 | The defense: adversarial training, results, and the tradeoff stated as a cost, not buried. |
| 17:30–20:00 | CI gate, limitations, one sentence placing this against prior NIDS evasion work, call to action (repo link, open issues). |
| 20:00–30:00 | Questions. |

### The question you don't want, answered anyway

**Q: "Isn't this attack kind of unrealistic? A real attacker doesn't get 200 free queries against your exact model."**

The deflecting answer sounds like: "Great question, that's definitely future work, but the main point still stands." Don't give that answer. Give this one:

> "Fair, and it's worse than 200 queries if the attacker can't get feedback at all — fully blind. What I actually tested is gray-box: the attacker knows the feature schema from the open-source repo but not the model weights, and gets a label back each query, which is realistic if they're attacking a honeypot they control to tune against before hitting the real target. I did not test a fully blind, no-feedback attacker, and I didn't test query-efficient black-box methods like HopSkipJump that would need far fewer than 200 queries. That's explicitly in my limitations section, and it's the first thing I'd do with another month."

That answer gives up nothing flattering and loses no credibility — it's more convincing than a hedge, because it shows the presenter already knows exactly where the result stops being load-bearing.

**Q: "Three seeds isn't a lot. How do you know 81.9% robust F1 isn't just noise?"**

> "It might be close to noise — across three seeds the robust F1 after adversarial training was 81.9%, 79.4%, and 83.1%, so call it 81.5% ± 1.9. The baseline's robust F1 without defense was 54.1%, 52.8%, 55.6%, so 54.2% ± 1.4. The gap between those two ranges doesn't overlap, so the direction of the effect holds, but I would not defend the exact 81.9% number in front of a statistician with only three seeds. Ten would make that interval tight enough to actually trust the magnitude, not just the sign."

Both answers follow the same shape: restate the critique accurately, give the real number even when it's unflattering, name exactly what wasn't tested, and point to a concrete next step instead of a vague "future work." That shape is reusable for any hard question in any defense — the content changes, the honesty pattern doesn't.

## 3. Finding and honestly positioning related work

The search, run for real against arXiv and Semantic Scholar:

```text
arXiv full-text search: "adversarial examples" "network intrusion detection"
arXiv full-text search: "adversarial robustness" "port scan"
Semantic Scholar: "adversarial robustness network intrusion detection",
  sorted by citation count, then by recency
```

That turns up a real, non-empty literature, which matters to know before writing a single word of the positioning paragraph:

- **IDSGAN** (Lin, Shi, Xue, 2018, arXiv:1809.02077) — a GAN that generates adversarial malicious traffic *records* to evade a black-box NIDS, evaluated on NSL-KDD.
- **Alhajjar, Maxwell, Bahnsen, 2020** (arXiv:2005.07519), "Evaluating and Improving Adversarial Robustness of Machine Learning-Based Network Intrusion Detectors" — GAN and search-based black-box evasion against several classifiers, also on NSL-KDD, plus a defense evaluation.
- **"SoK: Realistic Adversarial Attacks and Defenses for Intelligent Network Intrusion Detection"** (arXiv:2308.06819, 2023) — a systematization paper whose central argument is that most prior NIDS evasion work perturbs the *feature vector* without checking whether any real packet sequence maps to that perturbed vector — i.e., it's not clear the attacks are problem-space realizable at all.
- Two 2024 surveys, arXiv:2412.13880 and arXiv:2409.18736, mapping the field's open problems.

The SoK paper is the one to build the positioning paragraph around, because it names, in print, the exact gap the capstone's design decision fills:

> Prior adversarial-ML work against network intrusion detectors is substantial but almost entirely evaluated in feature space on canonical benchmark datasets — NSL-KDD and CICIDS2017 appear across the overwhelming majority of the papers I found, including IDSGAN (Lin et al., 2018) and Alhajjar et al. (2020), both of which perturb the already-extracted feature vector rather than the raw packets an attacker would send. The 2023 SoK on realistic adversarial attacks against NIDS makes this exact critique the center of its argument: most of this literature produces perturbations with no demonstrated path back to a real packet sequence. My contribution is narrow but real: I run the search directly over scan behavior — timing, port order, decoy count — that a real `nmap` invocation can produce, and feature values fall out as a consequence rather than the thing being optimized. I am not claiming a new attack family, and I am not claiming better numbers than Alhajjar et al.'s GAN-based approach against a harder, larger benchmark. I am claiming a complementary data point, on self-collected scan telemetry rather than the decade-old NSL-KDD, on a problem the SoK explicitly flags as under-studied, at a scale appropriate to a single-author capstone rather than a lab.

Three rules that paragraph follows, worth naming separately:

1. **Cite the strongest prior work you can find, not the weakest.** Citing only a weak 2015 paper to make a 2026 capstone look comparatively sophisticated is a credibility risk the moment one reader finds IDSGAN or the SoK themselves.
2. **State the difference in one concrete sentence per paper, not a hand-wave.** "Different from prior work" is not a sentence a reviewer or a sharp commenter will accept; "perturbs behavior, not the feature vector directly" is.
3. **If a survey or SoK paper already names the gap, cite it saying so.** It's the strongest form of positioning available to a small project — it means the "this is worth doing" case was already made by someone with no stake in this particular capstone.

This is the same move as the Portfolio Case Study written back in Tier 4, positioning the SBOM-gate contribution against Grype, Trivy, and Syft without claiming to replace any of them — and the same muscle used filing a PR against someone else's open-source project during the Open-Source Leadership Track: know exactly what's already there before claiming anything is new.

## Exercise: position your own magnum opus

Run the same search against your own capstone's intersection — not PortWatch-ML's, whatever two specializations your own magnum opus actually combines. Find at minimum one SoK/survey paper and two primary research papers on arXiv or Semantic Scholar that sit closest to what you built. Write the positioning paragraph: one sentence per paper on how your approach differs, and one explicit sentence on what you are *not* claiming.

Hint, not the paragraph itself: start from the survey or SoK paper, not a primary paper — it will usually hand you a ranked list of the field's most-cited work in its own related-work section, and often states the exact open gap your project happens to sit in, which is the fastest path to an honest, specific sentence instead of a vague one.

Get this paragraph right and the blog post, the talk's closing slide, and the arXiv preprint's Related Work section are the same three sentences, reformatted three times.
