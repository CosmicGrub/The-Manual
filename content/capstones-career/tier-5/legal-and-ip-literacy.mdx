## The Contract You Haven't Read Yet

Tier 0's Open-Source Etiquette & Licensing taught you to pick MIT, Apache-2.0, or GPL-3.0 for a project and justify the choice — permissive versus copyleft, Apache's patent grant, frictionless adoption versus enforced openness as the real tradeoff. That's a real skill, and you already have it. What it didn't teach you — because at Tier 0 you'd never shipped anything commercially, signed an offer letter, or had a maintainer hand you write access to a security-sensitive codebase — is what happens once license choice stops being a solo decision about your own repo and starts being a legal constraint on *combining* your code with someone else's, or a clause in a document someone else wrote that you're expected to sign without reading closely.

Career & Professional Soft Skills, the module immediately before this one, already walked you through a full freelance statement of work, the four contract-essentials clauses a freelance engagement needs (payment terms, IP ownership, scope creep, a kill clause), and a real invoice. This module doesn't repeat any of that — if you need the SOW or the invoice template, that's where they live. What's missing from your legal literacy isn't the freelance-engagement mechanics; it's everything that sits *around* that engagement and around full-time employment: whether you can legally combine two differently-licensed codebases, what you actually commit to when a maintainer asks you to sign a CLA, who owns code by default before any contract exists at all, what's actually patentable versus what isn't, the unsettled legal status of AI-generated code, what's worth flagging in an NDA or a non-compete before you sign it, how to read the IP-assignment clause in an employment offer letter, and what the law — not a scanning tool — actually requires of you if your product touches other people's personal data.

None of this makes you a lawyer. All of it makes you someone who reads before signing, asks a better question when something looks off, and knows which real document to pull up when a teammate, a client, or your own project needs one.

## What you'll leave this lesson knowing

- Why you generally cannot combine GPL-3.0 code into an MIT-licensed project without the whole combined work becoming GPL-3.0 — and why the reverse (MIT/Apache-2.0 into almost anything) is fine
- What dual licensing means and why a company might offer the same codebase under both a copyleft license and a paid commercial one
- What signing a Contributor License Agreement actually commits you to, and the real difference between a CLA (a license grant) and a full copyright assignment
- The default rule for who owns a copyrighted work, and why that default flips automatically for employees but not for independent contractors
- What's actually patentable (and why most ordinary application code isn't), plus the practical first step before committing to a product name
- Why the legal question of who owns AI-generated code is genuinely unsettled right now, and the one practical rule that applies regardless of how it resolves
- What's worth actually reading in an NDA and a non-compete before you sign either one, and why non-compete enforceability is not the same answer in every state
- How to read the IP-assignment clause in a real employment offer letter, and the narrower, real question of whether it reaches a side project built on your own time
- The difference between "what the law requires you to do" about user data (GDPR, CCPA, HIPAA) and "what a scanning tool checks" — a distinction DevOps Tooling's compliance content doesn't cover, because it's a legal-obligation question, not an infrastructure one

## Do this

1. **Work through a real license-compatibility problem: can you add a GPL-3.0 library to your MIT-licensed tool?** You already know, from Tier 0, that GPL-3.0 is copyleft and MIT is permissive. Here's the part Tier 0 didn't cover: what happens when you try to *combine* code under two different licenses in one project.

   Say you maintain a small, MIT-licensed command-line tool that converts scanned documents to text, and you want to add PDF parsing by pulling in a popular PDF-parsing library that happens to be licensed GPL-3.0. Can you do it and keep shipping your tool under MIT?

   No — not without changing your own project's license. Here's concretely *why*: GPL-3.0's copyleft obligation isn't a label on the library's own files, it's a condition on *distributing a combined or derivative work that incorporates GPL code*. The GPL's terms say that if you distribute a work built on GPL-licensed code, the whole distributed work must also be licensed under GPL-3.0, with source available. Linking your code against the GPL library, or bundling its source directly into your tool, makes your tool a work that incorporates GPL code — so the GPL's terms now apply to the *combination*, not just to the library's own files. Your options narrow to three: (a) relicense your entire tool as GPL-3.0 so the combination is compliant, (b) find an MIT- or Apache-2.0-licensed PDF-parsing library instead and avoid the problem entirely, or (c) use the GPL library only as a separate, standalone process your tool shells out to rather than linking against or bundling it (a real but legally nuanced workaround that depends on exactly how "combined work" gets interpreted for your specific case, and isn't a decision to make without being sure of the mechanism — when it's genuinely unclear, that's a case for a lawyer, not a guess).

   Now run the comparison the other direction: could you add an MIT-licensed or Apache-2.0-licensed library to a GPL-3.0 project, or to a closed-source proprietary one? Yes, with almost no restriction — this is the entire point of a permissive license. MIT and Apache-2.0 impose no copyleft obligation on the combined work, so their code can be folded into a GPL project, a proprietary product, or anything else, with the only real requirement being to keep the original copyright notice (and, for Apache-2.0, to preserve the patent-grant terms). This asymmetry — permissive code flows freely into anything, copyleft code pulls anything it touches toward itself — is the single fact that makes license compatibility checkable at all: before adding any dependency to a project you intend to distribute, you check whether that dependency's license is copyleft, and if it is, you check whether your own project's license is compatible with absorbing that obligation.

2. **Learn what dual licensing means, and why a company would offer the same codebase under two different licenses at once.** A dual-licensed project ships its source code under two separate license options simultaneously, and the user picks which one applies to their use. The real pattern you'll see most often: a copyleft license (usually GPL) for anyone willing to comply with copyleft, plus a separate paid commercial license for companies that want to use the code in a closed-source product without being forced to open-source what they build with it.

   MySQL's historical licensing model is a widely documented real example of this. MySQL AB offered the MySQL database engine under GPL for free, which worked fine for open-source projects and for companies willing to release their own product's source under GPL too. But a company that wanted to embed MySQL inside a proprietary, closed-source product couldn't do that under the GPL option — distributing their product would trigger the same copyleft propagation you just worked through in step 1. For those companies, MySQL AB sold a separate commercial license that granted the right to embed MySQL in a closed-source product without the GPL's share-source obligation, in exchange for a license fee.

   The business logic behind dual licensing is straightforward once you see it: the GPL option keeps the project genuinely open for the open-source community, while the commercial option monetizes exactly the use case the GPL was designed to make inconvenient — companies that want the code without the openness obligation. If you ever build something you intend to dual-license yourself, the one hard requirement is that you (or your organization) must hold the copyright to the *entire* codebase outright, with no third-party contributions under incompatible terms — which is also exactly why organizations that dual-license their own projects tend to require a CLA from every contributor, which is where step 3 picks up.

3. **Understand what signing a Contributor License Agreement (CLA) actually commits you to, and know the difference between a CLA and full copyright assignment.** When you open a pull request against some real projects, a bot blocks the merge until you sign a CLA. Read what you're actually agreeing to before you click through it, because "CLA" covers two meaningfully different kinds of document:

   - **A license-grant CLA** (the far more common form) has you keep your copyright on the contribution, but grants the project — or the organization that maintains it — a broad license to use, modify, sublicense, and redistribute your contribution, including under different license terms than the project's own published license, if the maintainers ever need to (for example, to dual-license the project the way step 2 described, or to relicense to a newer license version). You still own what you wrote; you've just given the project broad permission to do more with it than the project's public license alone would allow.
   - **A copyright-assignment agreement** is a meaningfully bigger ask: it transfers your actual copyright ownership of the contribution to the organization, not just a license to use it. The Free Software Foundation has historically required copyright assignment for contributions to official GNU project code, which is a real, well-documented example of this stronger form.

   Why do some real, well-known projects require a CLA and others explicitly don't? Projects under the Apache Software Foundation require a CLA (an Individual Contributor License Agreement, or a Corporate CLA for employer-sponsored contributions) before accepting any substantial contribution, and many corporate-backed open-source projects — plenty of Google-originated projects among them — require the same, typically enforced automatically by a CLA-check bot on the pull request. The reason is usually defensive: a CLA gives the maintaining organization clean, unambiguous legal standing to relicense, defend against a patent claim, or offer a dual-licensed commercial version later, without having to track down and get fresh permission from every past contributor. Other real projects explicitly avoid a CLA on principle — the Linux kernel is a well-documented example; instead of a CLA, it requires a "Signed-off-by" line under the Developer Certificate of Origin, which is a lighter-weight attestation that you have the right to submit the contribution under the project's existing license, without any separate grant or assignment of rights beyond that.

   The practical checklist before you sign anything: (1) read whether it's a license grant or a full copyright assignment — the words "assign," "transfer ownership," or "assigns all right, title, and interest" mean assignment; "grants a license" means you keep ownership; (2) check whether the grant is limited to the project's stated purpose or is unrestricted; (3) if you're contributing as part of your day job, check whether your employer's own IP-assignment clause (step 8) already claims that contribution before you have any CLA rights to grant in the first place — this is a real, concrete scenario if you're the learner from Upstream Maintainership who's earned Triage or Write access on a security-scanner project: before you sign that project's CLA, you'd want to be confident the contribution you're making is actually yours to license, and not something your employer's own offer-letter IP clause already claims.

4. **Learn the default rule for who owns a copyrighted work, and why employees and independent contractors are treated completely differently.** The default rule, absent any agreement or legal doctrine saying otherwise, is simple: whoever creates a work owns the copyright in it, automatically, the moment it's created — no registration, no filing, no contract required. Everything else in this module is really about the exceptions to that one default rule.

   The biggest exception is the work-made-for-hire doctrine under U.S. copyright law, and it treats employees and independent contractors completely differently:

   - **Employees:** work an employee creates within the scope of their employment is automatically owned by the employer, by operation of law, with no separate contract needed. If you write code as part of your day job, your employer owns it the moment you write it — this is why step 8's offer-letter IP clause matters so much: it's not creating a new right so much as making the existing default explicit (and, in the worst versions, trying to stretch it further than the default actually reaches).
   - **Independent contractors:** a contractor's work is *not* automatically work-for-hire, full stop — the work-for-hire doctrine for contractors only applies to a short, specific list of work categories defined in the Copyright Act (things like a contribution to a collective work, a translation, or test materials), and even then only with a signed written agreement saying so. Ordinary application code doesn't clearly fall on that list. In practice, this is exactly why a competent freelance contract doesn't just label the work "work made for hire" and stop — it includes an explicit IP-ownership/assignment clause that transfers ownership contractually, as a backstop, regardless of whether the work-for-hire label would hold up on its own. This is precisely the clause Career & Professional Soft Skills' freelance contract already has ("Client owns full rights to all deliverables... upon receipt of final payment") — now you know *why* that clause has to exist at all: without it, the freelancer, not the client, would own the copyright by default, no matter what anyone assumed.

5. **Learn what's actually patentable and why most application code isn't — and do a real trademark-search check before committing to a product name.** Patent and trademark protect two completely different things, and conflating them leads to wasted effort in both directions.

   **Patents** protect a novel, non-obvious, useful invention or process — not an idea, and not code as such. Most ordinary application code (a CRUD API, a UI component, a typical data pipeline) isn't patentable, because it's neither novel nor non-obvious in the patent-law sense, even if it took real skill to build. Software and business-method patents face real, well-documented extra scrutiny following *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International*, a real 2014 U.S. Supreme Court case that significantly narrowed what's eligible for a patent when an abstract idea (like a business method or a generic algorithm) is merely implemented on a generic computer rather than tied to some genuinely inventive technical improvement. Exactly where that line falls in any specific case is a fact-heavy, genuinely unsettled area of law even for specialists — this module won't pretend to draw it precisely, and neither should you without a patent attorney, if a real patent decision is ever actually on the table.

   **Trademarks** protect the name, logo, or other identifying mark of a product or company — not the underlying code or invention at all. The reason you'd trademark a project or product name is to stop someone else from using a confusingly similar name in the same market and to have a clear legal basis to make them stop. The practical first step, before you spend real effort building a brand around a name — a logo, a domain, marketing — is a basic trademark search: searching the USPTO's public trademark database for your candidate name (and close variants) in the relevant category of goods or services, to see whether someone has already registered or is already using it. This costs you nothing but a few minutes and catches the single most expensive mistake in naming a product: discovering the name is already taken only *after* you've built a brand around it, at which point acquiring the rights to keep using it — or rebranding everything you've already shipped — is vastly more expensive than the search would have been.

6. **Understand the real, unsettled legal question around IP ownership of AI-generated code, and the one practical rule that applies regardless of how it resolves.** AI/ML Tier 3's LLM Fundamentals module already taught you to read a model card and to distinguish "open-weight" from genuinely open-source or permissively licensed — Llama's community license is freely downloadable but carries real usage restrictions despite that. The same kind of gap between "looks open" and "is legally simple" shows up again here, in a different and higher-stakes place: who owns code an AI coding assistant generates, and is it even legal for the assistant to have been trained the way it was?

   There's a real, ongoing piece of litigation worth knowing by name: *Doe v. GitHub*, a filed class-action lawsuit alleging that GitHub Copilot's training on public GitHub code, and its output of code that in some cases closely reproduces training examples, violated the attribution and other terms of the open-source licenses that code was originally published under. This is genuinely unsettled — some claims in the case have been narrowed or dismissed as it has worked through the courts, while the central questions remain contested, and no court has issued a final, binding resolution on the underlying legal theory as of this writing. Describe it that way — "ongoing, unsettled, contested" — rather than as decided in either direction; it isn't, and claiming otherwise would be wrong regardless of which side you picked. The companies building and selling these tools generally argue that training a model on publicly available code is fair use — a transformative use that doesn't require a license from each original author — but that's an argument being made, not a legal conclusion that's been settled.

   Here's the part that matters for your actual job, and it doesn't wait for the lawsuit to resolve: check your employer's or client's actual AI-code-assistance policy *before* you ship AI-assisted code commercially. These policies already vary for real, concrete reasons that have nothing to do with how the litigation eventually comes out — some employers explicitly forbid any Copilot- or AI-assisted code in their proprietary codebase, full stop; others require you to disclose which lines or commits were AI-assisted; others allow it freely with no process at all. The uncertainty in the underlying law is exactly *why* organizations are writing their own policies now, rather than waiting years for case law to settle a question that's still open — if you ship AI-assisted code into a commercial codebase without checking which policy you're actually under, you're making a legal bet on your employer's behalf that they never agreed to.

7. **Learn what's worth reading in a real NDA before you sign it, and why non-compete enforceability depends entirely on which state you're in.** These are two of the most common documents a working engineer is actually handed to sign, and neither is covered by the freelance contract in Career & Professional Soft Skills.

   **NDAs (non-disclosure agreements)** come up constantly and for good reason — before a job interview that touches a company's unreleased product, before a client shares details of their system to scope your freelance work, before a partnership discussion. Most NDA language is boilerplate and fine. The one clause genuinely worth reading closely is the definition of "confidential information," because an overly broad one can bind you to more than it should. Here's realistic clause language worth flagging:

   > "Confidential Information means any and all information disclosed by the Disclosing Party to the Receiving Party, in any form, including but not limited to business plans, technical data, and any other information, without any requirement that such information be marked as confidential."

   The problem: this definition has no carve-out for information the receiving party already knew, information that's publicly available, or information they independently develop later without using the disclosed material — all standard, reasonable exclusions that a well-drafted NDA includes explicitly. Without that carve-out, a sufficiently aggressive reading could claim you're bound not to use or disclose something you already knew before the conversation ever happened, simply because it came up again during a disclosure covered by the NDA. A reasonable NDA either lists those exclusions explicitly ("Confidential Information does not include information that... (a) was already known to the Receiving Party prior to disclosure... (b) is or becomes publicly available through no fault of the Receiving Party...") or scopes the definition narrowly to information actually marked or identified as confidential. The second thing worth checking is duration — an NDA that binds you forever, with no end date, on information that will plainly be stale or public within a couple of years, is worth pushing back on; two to five years is far more typical for most business information.

   **Non-competes** restrict you from working for a competitor, or starting a competing venture, for some period after you leave a job. The single most important thing to know about them: enforceability is not the same answer everywhere, and "is this enforceable" has no universal yes/no. California is the clearest, most citable example of one extreme: California's Business and Professions Code §16600 statutorily voids almost all employee non-compete agreements, as a matter of state law — a California employer generally cannot enforce a non-compete against a former employee at all, with only narrow exceptions (like restrictions tied to the sale of a business). Many other U.S. states take the opposite default approach, enforcing non-competes if they're "reasonable" in scope, duration, and geography — a 12-month restriction limited to a specific narrow competitor set in a specific region might hold up in one of those states, while the exact same clause would simply be void on its face in California. The practical consequence: never assume a non-compete clause you're handed is either automatically enforceable or automatically worthless — the honest answer always starts with "it depends which state's law governs this," and if a real non-compete is actually in front of you with real stakes attached, that's a question for an employment lawyer licensed in the relevant state, not a guess from a general rule of thumb.

8. **Read a real employment offer letter's IP-assignment clause, and know the one real exception that protects your own side projects.** Nearly every full-time offer letter or employment agreement includes a clause assigning inventions and code you create to the employer. Realistic clause language:

   > "Employee agrees that all inventions, discoveries, and works of authorship, including all source code, conceived or developed by Employee during the term of employment, whether or not during working hours, shall be the sole and exclusive property of the Company."

   Read that clause the way you'd read any contract clause worth reading: what does it actually cover, versus what it says it covers? The phrase to look for is whether the assignment is limited to work "within the scope of employment" or related to the company's actual business and existing or anticipated work — versus a clause like the one above, which overreaches by claiming *anything* created during the employment relationship, "whether or not during working hours," with no subject-matter limit at all. Taken completely literally, a clause written that broadly could claim a side project you build nights and weekends on your own laptop, even if it has nothing to do with your employer's business.

   Here's the real, narrower protection that exists in some jurisdictions: California Labor Code §2870 is a real, well-documented statute that protects an employee's own invention from an employer's IP-assignment clause when the invention was developed entirely on the employee's own time, without using the employer's equipment, supplies, facilities, or trade secrets, *and* it doesn't relate to the employer's actual or demonstrably anticipated business, research, or development, *and* it didn't result from any work the employee performed for the employer. Notice how many conditions that is — all of them have to be true. A side project built on your own equipment and your own time, in a domain unrelated to your employer's business, is the kind of case this statute is designed to protect; a side project in the same domain as your employer's product, built with insights from your actual job, is not protected just because you worked on it at night. And §2870 is California-specific — other states have their own versions of this protection with their own exact wording, and some have none at all, which is exactly the same "it depends on the jurisdiction and the exact clause" lesson from step 7's non-compete section. The practical move, before you ever start a side project while employed full-time: read your own actual offer letter or employment agreement's IP clause, check whether your state has an equivalent statute, and if a side project is genuinely close to your employer's business, get it in writing (an explicit carve-out, in an email at minimum) rather than relying on a statute to settle an argument after the fact.

9. **Know what the law actually requires of a product owner handling user data — distinct from DevOps Tooling's scanning-tool compliance content.** DevOps Tooling's own compliance material covers the infrastructure side — SBOMs, dependency scanning, the tooling that checks your supply chain. This is the other half, the one that tooling can't check for you: what the law itself obligates you to do.

   - **GDPR** (the EU's General Data Protection Regulation) applies to anyone processing the personal data of people located in the EU, regardless of where the company processing that data is based — a U.S.-based startup with European users is covered, full stop, location of the company is irrelevant. Core obligations include having a lawful basis for processing personal data in the first place (consent is one, but not the only one), honoring a person's right to access the data held about them and their right to have it erased, and notifying the relevant supervisory authority of a personal-data breach within a defined short window (72 hours, under GDPR's breach-notification requirement) once the organization becomes aware of it.
   - **CCPA** (the California Consumer Privacy Act) is the closest U.S. analog, giving California residents rights to know what personal data is collected about them, to request deletion, and to opt out of the sale of their data — narrower in scope than GDPR and specific to California, but built on a similar philosophy of giving the individual rights over their own data.
   - **HIPAA** is U.S. health-data-specific and far narrower in who it actually covers than most people assume: it applies to "covered entities" (health plans, healthcare providers, healthcare clearinghouses) and their "business associates" who handle protected health information (PHI) in that regulated context. Most consumer apps are *not* HIPAA-covered, even if they handle sensitive personal information generally — a fitness-tracking app or a general wellness app, for instance, is typically not a HIPAA covered entity unless it's specifically built to handle PHI on behalf of a covered healthcare provider or plan. The practical move for any product owner: figure out, honestly, which of these actually applies based on *who your users are* and *what kind of data you're actually collecting* — not based on a vague sense that "we probably need to be compliant with something" — because each one imposes genuinely different, specific obligations, and assuming the wrong one (or assuming none apply when one does) is itself the mistake.

## Exercise

Using a real or plausible project of your own:

1. Pick one real, external dependency your project actually uses (or a library you'd plausibly add), look up its license, and write one paragraph on whether it's compatible with your own project's license — if your project is MIT or Apache-2.0 and the dependency is GPL, walk through exactly what would have to change (per step 1) for you to legally ship the combination.
2. Find one real NDA or employment offer letter you have access to (your own past offer letter, a template you can find publicly, or a plausible one you draft yourself) and read its confidentiality or IP-assignment clause closely. Write down, in your own words, exactly what it covers and whether it overreaches the way step 7 or step 8 described — and if you draft a plausible one, write it to be realistically overreaching on purpose, then flag the exact overreach.
3. Pick a real or plausible name for a project or product you might actually ship, and do a basic USPTO trademark search on it (or, if you don't have access, describe exactly what you'd search for and in which category of goods/services). Report what you'd find and whether the name looks clear to use.
4. For a real or plausible product idea that would collect user data, write two or three sentences identifying which of GDPR, CCPA, or HIPAA would actually apply to it, and why — based on who your users would be and what data you'd actually be collecting, not a guess.

**Hint, not a solution:** if step 1 feels abstract, pick the smallest real dependency in a real project you've already built in this curriculum (the Magnum Opus, a Tier 3 or Tier 4 project, anything with a `requirements.txt` or `package.json`) and check its actual license — most of the time the answer will be "this is already MIT or Apache-2.0 and there's no conflict," which is itself a useful, confidence-building result, not a disappointing one.
