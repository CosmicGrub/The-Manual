## Shipped Is Not the Same as Paid

The Magnum Opus Capstone made you design and ship a real, original project at the intersection of two specializations. Legal & IP Literacy, the module right before this one, made that project legally and defensibly *yours* — you now know why you can't casually fold a GPL-3.0 dependency into an MIT-licensed tool, what signing a CLA actually commits you to, and the default rule that means you, not some employer, own what you built on your own time. Career & Professional Soft Skills taught you how to negotiate a salary once an employer makes an offer, and how to run a single freelance engagement end to end once a client already said yes — a statement of work, a contract's four essential clauses, an invoice.

None of that is this module. Negotiating a salary assumes an employer. Running a freelance engagement assumes a client who already agreed to pay you for your time. This module assumes neither: you have a real, legally-clean *product* — an app, a small SaaS tool, a library with a paid tier — and nobody is going to pay for it unless you decide how it gets priced, where it gets sold, and who it's sold to, with no recruiter and no client-facing SOW doing that work for you. That's a genuinely different skill: pricing a product for a market of strangers instead of negotiating your own labor, and understanding the economics of the platforms and business models that stand between "I built this" and "people pay me for this." This module also won't re-derive what Legal & IP Literacy already covers — license choice and IP ownership are settled there; here, the question is purely commercial: now that it's legally yours to sell, how do you actually sell it.

## What you'll leave this lesson knowing

- The real commission structures on Apple's App Store and Google Play, including each platform's reduced-rate program for smaller developers, how those rates differ for subscriptions versus one-time in-app purchases, and at least one real, publicly documented friction point developers have raised about each platform
- The standard SaaS pricing-model taxonomy — flat-rate, tiered (good-better-best), per-seat, usage-based, and freemium — each illustrated with a real, currently-or-recently operating product that actually uses it
- The psychology behind a three-tier "decoy" pricing ladder and charm pricing ($X9 endings), stated with honest caveats about what's well-supported experimental evidence versus a single widely-retold study
- The real tradeoffs between a one-time purchase and a subscription for an indie app, and three named, real monetization patterns beyond app-store sales: sponsorware, paid open-core/dual-tier open source, and "build in public" as a go-to-market strategy
- How to compute Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC), Lifetime Value (LTV), and churn rate from real inputs, and why an LTV:CAC ratio of roughly 3:1 is the commonly-cited health target, not a hard law
- Why forming a business entity and handling sales-tax/VAT obligations is jurisdiction-specific in a way this module can only point at, not resolve — and why that's exactly the moment to pay a real accountant, not guess

## Do this

1. **Learn Apple's real App Store commission structure, including who actually qualifies for the reduced rate.** Apple's standard commission on paid app sales and in-app purchases is 30%. Since the App Store Small Business Program launched in late 2020, a developer whose App Store proceeds (what you actually receive, after Apple's own cut) were under $1 million in the prior calendar year qualifies for a reduced 15% commission instead — on essentially all of that developer's paid-app and in-app-purchase revenue, not just a sliver of it. If you cross the $1 million line mid-year, the standard 30% rate applies to the rest of that year, and you requalify for 15% the following year if you drop back under the threshold. Separately, and regardless of which program you're in, auto-renewable subscriptions get their own built-in discount: a subscription is commissioned at 30% during a subscriber's first year, then drops to 15% starting in that subscriber's second continuous year — a rule that's existed since 2016, independent of the Small Business Program. These specific numbers have held for several years as of this writing, but Apple's developer terms are exactly the kind of thing that changes without much warning — check the current App Store Small Business Program terms on Apple's own developer site before you build a financial model around any of these figures.

2. **Learn Google Play's commission structure and see where it genuinely differs from Apple's.** Google Play's standard commission is also 30%, but the reduced rate works differently from Apple's: since 2021, Google automatically applies a 15% service fee to the first $1 million (USD) of a developer's annual Play-billing revenue, with the standard 30% applying only to revenue above that threshold — and, unlike Apple's program, there's no revenue-based eligibility test to qualify for it — though developers still have to enroll via an account group and payments profile in Google Play Console to receive it, so it isn't automatically applied with zero setup either. Google also cut its subscription commission to 15% starting from day one of a subscription, not after a first-year grace period the way Apple's does. One more real, current wrinkle worth knowing exists, not memorizing exactly: Google has also been rolling out a more granular fee structure (varying by whether a transaction comes from a "new" or "existing" user, among other factors) region by region, including changes scheduled for major markets in 2026 — which is exactly why, before pricing a real Android app or in-app purchase, you check Google Play Console's own current service-fee documentation rather than trusting a number you read somewhere once.

3. **Know at least one real, publicly documented friction point developers have raised about each platform, and understand that recent policy changes are often region-specific and still moving.** This isn't trivia — it affects what's actually possible to build, and where. On anti-steering (restricting apps from telling users about cheaper ways to pay outside the app), *Epic Games v. Apple* is the real, named case: Epic sued Apple in 2020 over App Store policies, and the long-running litigation forced real changes. As of this writing, the core fight over whether Apple must let apps link out to external payment pages is actually settled — the Ninth Circuit's December 2025 ruling stood after the Supreme Court declined to hear Apple's appeal and a subsequent rehearing request was denied in March 2026 — but what Apple can still charge on those external, off-platform purchases is not: Apple is currently charging roughly 27% on sales made through external links, a rate developers have continued to publicly push back on as functionally undermining the point of linking out at all. Separately and more significantly for anyone building specifically for the EU: the EU's Digital Markets Act designated Apple as a "gatekeeper," and — in the EU specifically, not globally — Apple has had to allow alternative app marketplaces and alternative in-app payment processors on iOS. The European Commission fined Apple €500 million in April 2025 for continued anti-steering practices; Apple's response, as of October 2026, was to abolish its flat per-install "Core Technology Fee" entirely and replace it with a 5% "Core Technology Commission" on transactions through alternative marketplaces or off-platform payments, plus a tiered in-app-purchase commission depending on which payment path a developer uses (roughly 26% through Apple's own payment system, 20% through an alternative processor, or 15% when simply linking out to an external website) — terms that only took effect October 1, 2026, with the Commission having already opened a further non-compliance investigation into Apple's fee structure back in January 2026. The honest takeaway: this is not a settled picture anywhere, the EU's numbers specifically changed within the last few weeks of this module being written, and the only safe move is checking each platform's current developer terms for the specific region you're actually shipping into — not trusting any number above, or anywhere else, as permanent.

4. **Learn the five standard SaaS pricing models, each with one real product that actually uses it.** This is the taxonomy nearly every SaaS pricing conversation reduces to:
   - **Flat-rate** — one price, usually for a fixed bundle of features or usage, regardless of team size. Basecamp is a long-cited real example: for years it sold a single flat-rate "Pro Unlimited" tier (unlimited projects and users for one flat monthly price) specifically as an alternative to the complexity of per-seat billing, alongside a separate per-user "Plus" plan for smaller teams — a real product offering both a flat-rate and a per-seat option side by side, which is itself worth noticing.
   - **Tiered / "good-better-best"** — multiple named tiers (commonly three or four), each unlocking more features, usage, or limits than the one before. Zoom is a real, recognizable example: a free Basic tier with real limits (a capped meeting duration for group calls), then Pro, Business, and Enterprise tiers that progressively raise participant counts and add admin/security features.
   - **Per-seat / per-user** — price scales with the number of people using the product. Slack is a commonly-cited real example, and specifically bills based on *active* users during the billing period rather than every account ever created, a detail Slack itself has marketed as a fairness feature.
   - **Usage-based / metered** — price scales with actual consumption rather than seats. Twilio is a real, standard example: you're charged per message sent or per minute of call handled, not a flat monthly fee, so cost rises and falls directly with how much of the product you actually use. (Exact per-unit rates vary by country and change often — the pricing *model*, not the specific cents, is the durable lesson here.)
   - **Freemium** — a genuinely usable free tier, with a paid tier unlocking more. Canva is a real example: the free tier is a complete, usable design tool, while Canva Pro unlocks a larger asset/template library, brand-kit features, and background-removal-style tools — the free tier isn't a crippled demo, it's a real product that's also a funnel into the paid one.

   Notice that more than one of these can coexist in a single real product (Basecamp openly sells both flat-rate and per-seat plans) — the taxonomy describes patterns, not mutually exclusive categories a company has to pick exactly one of.

5. **Understand the psychology behind pricing-page design, honestly — without overclaiming it as settled science.** Two patterns show up constantly on real pricing pages:
   - **Anchoring via a three-tier "decoy."** The most-repeated version of this idea comes from behavioral economist Dan Ariely's account of a real subscription offer he found for *The Economist*: a web-only subscription, a print-only subscription at the same price as a combined print-and-web option, and the print-and-web option itself. The print-only option, priced identically to print-plus-web, made almost nobody choose it — but its presence made the combined option look like an obvious bargain by comparison, and more people chose the (more expensive) combined tier than when the print-only "decoy" wasn't offered at all. Be honest about what this is: it's a widely retold classroom demonstration, not a large, independently replicated controlled trial, and the broader claim it illustrates — that adding a dominated, unappealing option changes what people choose among the *other* options — is a real, broadly supported idea in behavioral economics (it's often called the "decoy effect" or asymmetric dominance), even though the exact Economist anecdote shouldn't be cited as rigorously proven on its own. The practical version that shows up on SaaS pricing pages constantly: build three tiers, make the middle one the one you actually want most customers to pick, and price the top tier so the middle one looks like the obviously reasonable choice by comparison.
   - **Charm pricing (prices ending in 9).** Prices like $29 or $49 instead of $30 or $50 are extremely common on real pricing pages, and there's real, peer-reviewed research behind why: a well-known field experiment on actual mail-order sales (Anderson & Simester) found that raising a price from $34 to $39 *increased* sales rather than decreasing them — evidence for what researchers call "left-digit bias," where people anchor disproportionately on the leftmost digit of a price and read $39 as meaningfully cheaper than $40, more cheaply than the one-dollar difference would suggest. This one has real experimental support across multiple studies, which is a different confidence level than the decoy anecdote above — but it's still a tendency observed in aggregate consumer behavior, not a guarantee for any specific product or audience, and it's worth testing on your own pricing page rather than assuming it transfers automatically.

6. **Weigh one-time purchase versus subscription for an indie app, using real, named examples of indie developers who made each choice deliberately.** A one-time purchase is simple to understand and sell, but caps your revenue per customer and gives you no ongoing revenue to fund continued development once the initial sales taper off. A subscription produces recurring revenue that can fund ongoing work, but asks a user to keep re-justifying the expense every billing cycle, and indie developers who've switched from one to the other have publicly taken real reputational heat for it — the writing app Ulysses's 2017 move from a one-time purchase to a subscription is a well-documented real example that triggered visible public backlash from existing users who'd already paid once and didn't expect to pay again. Other real indie apps have deliberately stayed one-time-purchase as a point of philosophy and market positioning — Things 3, a well-known task-manager app, has long marketed itself partly *on* not having a subscription, accepting a lower recurring-revenue ceiling in exchange for that positioning. Neither choice is objectively correct; it's a real tradeoff between predictable recurring revenue and the trust cost of asking an existing customer to start paying again for something they already bought once.

7. **Learn how sponsorware and the real sponsorship platforms behind it actually work, not just that they exist.** "Sponsorware" is a real, named pattern — associated with developer Caleb Porzio, who practiced it for his Alpine.js and Livewire projects (no source confirms he coined the term itself, but he's the developer most prominently linked to using it) — where a developer builds a feature or a small tool and makes it available first, and only, to their financial sponsors, then open-sources it publicly after some delay (days, weeks, or until a funding goal is hit). It's not the same as simply asking for donations: sponsors get something real and exclusive, if temporary, in exchange for the money, which is the part that actually drives sign-ups. The two platforms that make this and plain OSS sponsorship practical: **GitHub Sponsors** lets anyone sponsor a developer or organization directly from a GitHub profile, as a one-time or recurring monthly payment, with GitHub itself charging no platform fee when the sponsorship is funded by an individual (payment-processor fees still apply) — though since mid-2023 GitHub does take a real 3% (bank transfer) or 6% (credit card) cut specifically on sponsorships funded by an organization account, a real exception worth knowing if your sponsor is a company rather than a person — note that GitHub ran a Sponsors Matching Fund that matched sponsorship income for newly-enrolled developers, but that fund closed to new applicants years ago, so don't plan around it existing today. **Open Collective** works differently: it's a fiscal-hosting platform, meaning a project without its own legal business entity can receive and transparently spend money (every transaction is published on a public ledger) through a fiscal host (commonly the Open Source Collective, a nonprofit that handles the legal/tax plumbing) in exchange for a platform fee — it's the better fit for a project or small collective that wants transparent, pooled community funding rather than money flowing to one individual's personal account.

8. **Understand paid open-core as a real, named dual-tier open-source business model — and know exactly where this stops and Legal & IP Literacy's territory starts.** Open-core means a genuinely open-source "core" edition is free, while a separate, usually proprietary "enterprise" edition adds paid features (SSO, advanced admin controls, compliance tooling) on top of that same codebase. GitLab is a real, well-documented example: GitLab Community Edition is open-source, while GitLab Enterprise Edition layers additional paid tiers (historically branded Premium/Ultimate) with extra features on the same underlying product. This module only covers *why* a company would structure a product this way commercially — which features to gate behind a paid tier, and how that funds continued development of the free core. It deliberately does not re-cover *how* you'd actually structure the licensing to make that split legally sound (which license the core uses, how a CLA lets you later relicense or dual-license contributed code, what obligations a copyleft core license would impose on a closed-source "enterprise" layer) — that's exactly the ground Legal & IP Literacy already walked through, and this module isn't repeating it.

9. **Learn "build in public" as a real, named go-to-market pattern — not as motivational hype.** Building in public means an indie developer or small team publicly shares real metrics as they go — revenue numbers, user counts, churn, what broke, what didn't work — usually on a blog or a platform like X/Twitter, instead of staying quiet until a polished launch. The mechanism, concretely, is that transparency itself becomes the marketing: an audience follows the numbers and the honest story over time, develops trust in the builder before the product is even finished, and converts into early customers and word-of-mouth once it ships, because they've effectively watched it get built. Indie maker Pieter Levels is a real, frequently-cited example of this pattern at the individual level, having built and publicized multiple profitable small products (Nomad List and Remote OK among them) largely through public, ongoing updates on his own progress and revenue rather than traditional marketing spend. At company scale, Buffer is a real, well-documented example that went further than most: it launched a public transparency dashboard in April 2020 covering real business metrics, including revenue — an unusually extreme version of the same underlying idea, though check whether it's still actively maintained before citing its live numbers, since a dashboard like this is exactly the kind of thing a company can quietly retire without much announcement. The pattern works because the "marketing" is specific, verifiable information shared consistently over time, not vague hype — which is also exactly why it fails if the numbers you're sharing turn out to be exaggerated or cherry-picked; the entire mechanism depends on the audience trusting what you post is real.

10. **Learn to compute CAC, LTV, churn, and the LTV:CAC ratio, with a full worked numeric example.** These four numbers are the minimum unit-economics vocabulary for deciding whether a product's growth spending is actually sustainable. Definitions first:
    - **Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)** — total sales-and-marketing spend over a period, divided by the number of new customers acquired in that period.
    - **Churn rate** — the fraction of customers who cancel in a given period (commonly measured monthly for a subscription product).
    - **Lifetime Value (LTV)** — the average revenue you expect from a customer over their entire relationship with you. A common simplified formula for a subscription product: `LTV = (average monthly revenue per customer) ÷ (monthly churn rate)`.
    - **LTV:CAC ratio** — LTV divided by CAC, commonly cited as healthy at roughly 3:1 or higher — meaning a customer is worth at least about three times what it cost to acquire them. This is a widely-repeated industry heuristic, not a law of economics; a ratio below roughly 3:1 is a real warning sign that growth spending may not be sustainable, but the "right" ratio varies by business model, margin structure, and how fast you need to grow.

    **Worked example** (illustrative numbers, picked to make the arithmetic clean — the method is what matters):

    Say your Magnum Opus has become a small subscription SaaS tool at $20/month per customer. Last quarter, you spent $4,000 total on ads and content marketing, and that spending brought in 40 new paying customers. Of your existing customer base, 5% canceled in an average month.

    - **CAC** = $4,000 ÷ 40 new customers = **$100 per customer**.
    - **LTV** = $20 average monthly revenue ÷ 0.05 monthly churn rate = **$400 per customer** (meaning the average customer sticks around roughly 20 months — `1 ÷ 0.05 = 20` — and pays $20/month across that span: `20 × $20 = $400`, matching the formula).
    - **LTV:CAC ratio** = $400 ÷ $100 = **4:1**.

    That 4:1 ratio sits comfortably above the commonly-cited 3:1 health target, which means — on these illustrative numbers — the $100 it costs to acquire a customer is being paid back roughly four times over across that customer's lifetime, suggesting the current marketing spend is working and could plausibly be scaled up, not just sustained. Change any one input — say churn rises from 5% to 10% a month — and LTV drops to $20 ÷ 0.10 = $200, pushing the ratio down to 2:1, below the healthy target, with no change at all to how much you're spending on ads. That sensitivity is the real lesson: churn, not acquisition spend, is very often the number that silently breaks the economics of a small subscription product.

11. **Know that business-structure and sales-tax/VAT obligations are real, jurisdiction-specific, and genuinely outside what this module can responsibly tell you.** The moment you start actually taking money for a product — whether you operate as a sole proprietor, form an LLC, or incorporate some other way — you take on real obligations that vary by country and, within the U.S., by state: whether and how to register a business entity, whether you owe sales tax to U.S. states (a real, live obligation since the U.S. Supreme Court's 2018 *South Dakota v. Wayfair* decision, which allows states to require sales-tax collection based on economic activity in the state even without a physical presence there), and whether EU customers trigger VAT obligations on digital goods and services sold to them (a real, long-standing requirement under EU VAT rules for digital products, collected differently than U.S. sales tax and administered through its own registration/reporting mechanisms). This module, like Legal & IP Literacy before it, can only tell you honestly that these obligations exist and vary by exactly where you and your customers are — it cannot tell you which specific structure or registration applies to your specific situation, because that genuinely depends on your country, your state or region, your revenue level, and where your customers are located. The real, responsible move before you start actually charging real customers real money: talk to an actual accountant and, if the stakes are high enough, a lawyer, in your own jurisdiction — exactly the same honest hedge Legal & IP Literacy already modeled for jurisdiction-specific legal questions, applied here to jurisdiction-specific financial and tax ones.
